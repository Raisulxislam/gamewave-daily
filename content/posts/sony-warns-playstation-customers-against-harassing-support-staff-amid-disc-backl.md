## Sony Draws a Line at Customer Abuse

Sony Japan published a new customer harassment policy on September 1, and the timing tells the whole story. Ever since the company announced on July 1, 2026 that physical PlayStation disc production would end in January 2028, the backlash has been relentless — petitions, a coordinated #PSBlackout boycott in late August, and a €400 million Dutch consumer lawsuit. But the fury didn't stay online: customer service agents, who had zero say in the decision, absorbed the worst of it.

The new policy formally defines "customer harassment" as behavior that threatens the "dignity and safety" of employees. It includes persistent demands, abusive language, threats, intimidation, discriminatory or sexual conduct, and even unauthorized visits to Sony offices. Crucially, it also flags "threats to go to the media" as potentially actionable behavior — a clause that has drawn skepticism from critics who see it as a way to silence legitimate complaints.

## What Happens if You Violate

Sony's stated consequences are blunt: customer service can be suspended or terminated without notice, and in serious cases the company may pursue legal action or criminal proceedings with law enforcement. The policy aligns with Japan's revised Act on Comprehensive Promotion of Labor Measures, which takes effect October 1, 2026 and mandates all companies to implement anti-harassment protections for workers. Square Enix and Capcom have rolled out similar policies in recent months.

There's a real tension here. Legitimate protest — the class-action lawsuit, the boycotts, the social media campaigns — doesn't constitute harassment under Sony's own framework. But the vague language around "prolonged demands" and "requests unrelated to products or services" has left room for concern that the policy could be wielded against vocal but lawful critics. Sony's biggest challenge now isn't managing the disc backlash — it's convincing customers the new rules protect workers without muzzling them.
